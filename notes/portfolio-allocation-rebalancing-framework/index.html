Portfolio Allocation and Rebalancing: A Practical Framework

A practical way to think about what each investment is doing in a portfolio, how allocations drift and when a review may be useful.

Published: 2026-09-28 · Updated: 2026-09-28 · 6 min read

Allocation comes before product selection

Portfolio allocation is the decision about how much of a portfolio is exposed to different asset classes or investment roles. The appropriate mix depends on the goal, time horizon and risk tolerance; there is no universal allocation that fits everyone.

Investor.gov describes asset allocation as dividing investments among asset classes such as stocks, bonds and cash, with the mix influenced by time horizon and risk tolerance.

Think in roles

Instead of treating every holding as an isolated decision, assign each part of the portfolio a role. For example, a growth allocation may seek long-term appreciation, while a liquidity allocation may exist to meet near-term needs. The exact categories should reflect the investor's own plan.

Measure drift

Market movements change portfolio weights. If one allocation grows faster than another, the portfolio can become materially different from the intended structure even when no new trade was made.

A simple review compares the current weight with the target weight and records the difference. That creates an objective starting point for deciding whether a rebalance is needed.

Rebalancing is a rule, not a prediction

Rebalancing restores a portfolio toward its intended structure. A policy can use a calendar review, a percentage-band trigger, or a combination. The important part is to define the rule before emotions or recent performance determine the decision.

Investor.gov notes that rebalancing can be considered when holdings move out of alignment with investment goals and describes both periodic and threshold-based approaches.

Investor.gov: Asset Allocation and Diversification

A practical review checklist

  1. What is the goal and time horizon?
  2. What role does each major allocation serve?
  3. What is the current percentage allocation?
  4. How far has each allocation moved from its intended range?
  5. Has the underlying investment thesis or role changed?
  6. Can new contributions correct part of the drift without selling?
  7. What taxes, fees or trading constraints should be considered?

What this framework does not do

It does not predict markets or identify a universally correct target allocation. It is a structure for making portfolio reviews more deliberate and repeatable.
