US Stocks and ETFs: A Practical Starting Point

A simple framework for understanding the difference between owning individual companies and using exchange-traded funds before thinking about portfolio construction.

Published: 2026-09-28 · Updated: 2026-09-28 · 6 min read

Start with the exposure, not the ticker

A stock represents an ownership interest in a company. An ETF is a pooled investment product that can hold stocks, bonds or other assets and trades on an exchange. The important first question is what exposure you are trying to obtain, rather than which ticker looks attractive.

Individual stocks

Owning an individual company creates concentrated exposure to that business. Research therefore needs to consider the company's business model, financial condition, valuation, competitive position and risks. A good company can still be a poor investment at an unsuitable price.

ETFs and index funds

Many ETFs provide exposure to a basket of securities. Index funds are designed to track an index rather than select securities with the goal of outperforming it. A broad fund can simplify diversification, but a narrowly focused ETF is not automatically diversified.

A simple comparison checklist

  • Exposure: What companies, sectors, countries or asset classes does it actually hold?
  • Concentration: How much depends on a small number of holdings?
  • Costs: What fees and transaction costs apply?
  • Risk: What could make the investment fall materially?
  • Time horizon: Does the investment fit the period for which the money can remain invested?
  • Portfolio role: What does this holding add that the rest of the portfolio does not?

Why diversification matters

Diversification spreads exposure across investments rather than depending on one company or narrow segment. It can reduce concentration risk, but it cannot prevent losses when markets broadly decline.

For a deeper explanation, see the Investor.gov asset allocation and diversification guide.

A useful next step

Before researching individual stocks, write down the portfolio role you want the investment to play. This turns research from “Is this a good stock?” into a more useful question: “Does this investment fit the portfolio I am trying to build?”
