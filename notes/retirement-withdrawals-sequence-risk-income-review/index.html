Retirement Withdrawals: Sequence Risk, Income and Annual Reviews

After retirement begins, the challenge changes from building a corpus to funding spending through uncertain markets, inflation, taxes and lifespan. A clear withdrawal process makes the assumptions and trade-offs easier to review.

Published: 2026-10-12 · Updated: 2026-10-13 · 8 min read

Retirement has two different financial phases

Before retirement, contributions and investment growth may be the main drivers of progress. After retirement, money also leaves the portfolio to pay for living costs. That change means the order of returns can matter, not just the average return across a long period.

A withdrawal plan should start with spending needs, income that is reasonably dependable, the assets available to fund the gap, and the time horizon the portfolio may need to support. It should not begin with a product or a single percentage assumed to work for every household.

Why the sequence of returns matters

Two portfolios can experience the same set of annual returns but finish with different balances when withdrawals happen between those returns. Losses early in retirement can be particularly difficult because withdrawals may require selling assets after they have fallen, leaving less capital available for a recovery.

The following simplified illustration uses a starting portfolio of ₹100 lakh and a ₹10 lakh withdrawal at the end of each year. The returns are hypothetical and deliberately simple; the example excludes taxes, fees, inflation and any other cash flows.

ScenarioYear 1After withdrawalYear 2After withdrawal
Gain then loss₹100L × 1.20 = ₹120L₹110L₹110L × 0.80 = ₹88L₹78L
Loss then gain₹100L × 0.80 = ₹80L₹70L₹70L × 1.20 = ₹84L₹74L

Both paths contain a 20% gain and a 20% loss. With withdrawals after each year's return, the ending balances differ. This is not a forecast or a comparison of investment products; it demonstrates how cash flows interact with return order.

Map spending before deciding how to fund it

Separate the spending the household expects to fund from the income and assets available to meet it. The categories below are a planning checklist, not a recommendation to buy a particular product.

  • Essential spending: housing, food, utilities, routine healthcare and other core costs.
  • Flexible spending: discretionary expenses that could be adjusted temporarily if conditions require it.
  • Other known commitments: planned repairs, family support or other large expenses that should not be hidden inside a monthly average.
  • Income sources: pension, annuity, rental income or other sources only to the extent their amount, start date, reliability, tax treatment and inflation sensitivity are understood.
  • Portfolio funding gap: the amount that remains after relevant income is compared with spending for the same period.

Do not treat an uncertain income stream as guaranteed, or count emergency reserves and assets earmarked for another goal as if they were all available for routine retirement withdrawals.

Map the funding gap before choosing a withdrawal approach

A simple cash-flow example makes the starting question concrete. Suppose a hypothetical household estimates annual retirement spending of ₹9 lakh and has ₹3 lakh a year of income it considers reasonably dependable. The initial gap to plan for is ₹6 lakh a year (₹9 lakh − ₹3 lakh), before considering tax, timing, inflation, irregular expenses or changes in income.

This arithmetic does not say that a portfolio can safely fund the gap, and it does not recommend a withdrawal rate or product. The next step is to test how the gap changes under higher spending, lower income and different return assumptions, then review whether the plan still fits the household’s circumstances.

A withdrawal percentage is not a guarantee

A percentage calculated against the initial portfolio can be easy to communicate, but it does not prove that the money will last. Sustainability depends on the starting balance, spending pattern, inflation, investment returns, fees, taxes, asset mix, and how long withdrawals continue.

A fixed nominal withdrawal, an inflation-adjusted withdrawal and a flexible withdrawal linked to portfolio conditions are different models. Each produces different spending stability and portfolio risk. A simplified model can help compare assumptions, but it cannot guarantee a safe outcome for an individual household.

Be especially careful when applying a withdrawal-rate study to another country or household. Market history, product access, taxation, inflation, retirement benefits and the study's assumptions may differ. Treat research as context to investigate, not a ready-made rule.

Build a practical review routine

A retirement withdrawal plan is a process that should be reviewed when material circumstances change. An annual review can include:

  1. Compare actual spending with the plan and explain any persistent difference.
  2. Update dependable income, its start dates and any changes in reliability.
  3. Review the portfolio value and the amount withdrawn since the last review.
  4. Check whether inflation, market conditions, fees or taxes have changed the funding gap.
  5. Revisit the expected retirement horizon and known large expenses.
  6. Test a lower-return or higher-spending scenario rather than relying only on a central estimate.
  7. Document the reason for any change to spending or the withdrawal approach.

A review should be based on the plan and the household's circumstances, not on reacting automatically to every short-term market move.

Keep rules and product terms current

Withdrawal, tax and exit rules depend on the specific account or product and can change. For India-specific NPS questions, consult the PFRDA NPS All Citizen Model information and the applicable current regulations before acting. Do not assume that rules for an overseas retirement account apply in India.

For a general conceptual introduction to planning withdrawals and lifetime income, see the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's Investor.gov retirement income guidance. It is background reading, not Indian tax or product guidance.

Where rules, tax consequences or product terms materially affect a decision, verify the current primary documents and consider a qualified professional who is appropriate to your circumstances.

Connect the withdrawal plan to the corpus estimate

The corpus estimate and the withdrawal plan answer related but different questions. A corpus model estimates what a defined spending stream might require under stated assumptions. A withdrawal process explains how the household will monitor that stream and respond when real spending, returns or income differ from the model.

Start with the retirement corpus calculation framework, then compare its assumptions with the withdrawal plan. The retirement planning calculator can help with arithmetic, but its outputs remain only as reliable as the inputs and model limitations.
