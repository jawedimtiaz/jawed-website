Choosing AI Tools: Privacy, Cost and Practical Fit

A simple framework for evaluating an AI tool before putting it into a personal or workplace workflow.

Published: 2026-09-29 · Updated: 2026-09-29 · 6 min read

Start with the task, not the product

Different tasks can have different requirements. Before comparing products, describe the job: writing, summarizing, research, coding, image work, transcription, automation or another specific use.

The goal is to find a tool that fits the task and the surrounding requirements, not to assume that the newest or most capable product is automatically the right choice.

Six questions to ask

  1. Does it solve the task? Test the actual workflow rather than relying only on feature lists.
  2. What data will it receive? Identify whether the task involves personal, confidential, customer, employer or otherwise restricted information.
  3. What controls exist? Check the provider's current privacy, retention, sharing, training and administrator controls.
  4. What does it cost? Consider subscription cost, usage limits and any additional service or API charges that apply.
  5. How will output be checked? Decide what a human needs to verify before the result is used.
  6. What happens if the tool changes? Check whether the workflow depends on a feature, model, integration or policy that could change.

Use a simple evaluation table

AreaQuestion
Task fitDoes it reliably help with the specific job?
DataWhat information would be entered or uploaded?
PrivacyWhat current controls and policies apply?
CostWhat will the actual workflow cost?
QualityHow will important output be reviewed?
AccessDoes the organization permit the tool and intended use?
ContinuityCould a product, feature or policy change disrupt the workflow?

Keep sensitive work inside the right boundary

Do not assume that a public AI service is approved for workplace information simply because it is technically possible to upload the data. Follow the organization's policies, data-classification rules and approved-tool requirements.

For higher-risk use cases, use the provider's current official documentation and your organization's security or privacy guidance rather than relying on a generic article.

Use official sources for current controls

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework provides a voluntary framework for organizations to manage AI risks and includes a generative-AI profile. It can be useful as a reference when thinking about governance and risk rather than treating an AI product as a black box. NIST AI Risk Management Framework →

Product-specific privacy and data controls can change. For example, ChatGPT has current data-control documentation describing available privacy and model-improvement settings. OpenAI Data Controls →

A low-risk starting approach

  1. Choose a task with a clear, reviewable outcome.
  2. Use non-sensitive or sanitized information for the first test.
  3. Compare the result with your existing workflow.
  4. Record what still requires human review.
  5. Check the current provider and organizational policies before expanding use.
