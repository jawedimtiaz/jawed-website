Retirement Planning: Start With the Number

Retirement planning becomes easier to reason about when you start with the spending you want to fund, the years you have to prepare, and the assumptions behind the target.

Published: 2026-09-28 · Updated: 2026-09-28 · 7 min read

Retirement planning is a funding problem

A retirement plan has two broad phases: building resources before retirement and using those resources after employment income stops or falls. The first useful question is therefore not “Which investment should I buy?” but “What future spending does my retirement plan need to support?”

A retirement target is an estimate, not a guaranteed future number. It should be revisited as income, spending, family responsibilities, retirement age, inflation and investment assumptions change.

Step 1: Estimate retirement spending

Start with today's annual household spending and separate it into categories that are likely to continue, change or disappear after retirement.

CategoryPlanning question
Essential living costsWhat spending is required for housing, food, utilities and routine needs?
HealthcareWhat recurring healthcare and insurance costs should be allowed for?
Family commitmentsWhich education, marriage or support commitments will still exist at retirement?
Discretionary spendingWhat travel, hobbies, gifts or lifestyle spending do you want to preserve?
Taxes and irregular costsWhat taxes, repairs, replacements or other non-monthly costs need a reserve?

It is useful to distinguish retirement spending from current spending. Some costs may fall, while others—especially healthcare or leisure—may rise.

Step 2: Choose a retirement date and horizon

The time until retirement affects how long contributions can compound. The expected retirement horizon affects how long the corpus may need to fund withdrawals.

For a simple first model, define four numbers: current age, planned retirement age, expected lifespan for planning purposes, and the years between today and retirement. These are assumptions, not predictions.

Step 3: Account for inflation

A retirement budget stated in today's rupees will generally not buy the same basket of goods decades later. A planning model therefore needs an inflation assumption.

A simple future-spending estimate can be represented as:

Future annual spending = Current annual spending × (1 + inflation rate)years to retirement

This is a planning formula, not a forecast. Actual inflation can vary substantially across categories and over time.

Step 4: Define the retirement corpus carefully

The retirement corpus is the pool of assets intended to support future spending and other retirement needs. A useful model should distinguish between assets that are genuinely available for retirement and assets already earmarked for other goals.

For example, money reserved for a child's education, a home purchase, or another near-term obligation should not automatically be counted as retirement capital merely because it appears on the same balance sheet.

Step 5: Use assumptions instead of a single magic number

There is no universal retirement corpus number. The result depends on the retirement age, spending level, inflation, investment return, taxes, withdrawal pattern, longevity and the assets included in the calculation.

A better starting model shows a range of scenarios rather than pretending that one assumed return or lifespan is certain.

  • Conservative case: higher spending or inflation and lower real returns.
  • Base case: central planning assumptions that you can explain and review.
  • Stress case: an adverse sequence such as higher early-retirement inflation or weaker portfolio returns.

Step 6: Separate retirement assets from other goals

Retirement is often only one goal competing for the same savings. A practical plan can maintain separate goal buckets or at least separate accounting for retirement, education, emergency reserves, major purchases and other commitments.

This prevents a large total net-worth figure from creating a false impression that all of it is available to fund retirement.

Where pensions and retirement accounts fit

India has multiple retirement and pension arrangements. NPS is one example: PFRDA describes the All Citizen Model as a retirement savings account with withdrawals governed by current PFRDA rules. Current PFRDA material also notes that NPS rules and exit provisions have been amended, including amendments issued in July 2026.

The important planning principle is to treat the retirement account or pension product as one component of the overall plan. Its contribution limits, tax treatment, investment choices, liquidity and exit rules should be checked against current official rules rather than assumed from an older article.

A simple retirement-planning worksheet

  1. Record current annual household spending.
  2. Remove costs that are expected to end before retirement.
  3. Add retirement-specific costs that may increase.
  4. Choose a planning retirement age and a planning lifespan.
  5. Choose explicit inflation and return assumptions.
  6. Project retirement-year spending.
  7. Estimate the corpus required to support the planned retirement period.
  8. List retirement-designated assets separately from other goals.
  9. Compare the projected corpus with the target and identify the gap.
  10. Review the assumptions periodically instead of treating the first calculation as permanent.

What this first calculation does—and does not—tell you

A retirement target is useful because it turns a vague goal into something that can be monitored. It does not tell you which specific security, fund, pension product or asset allocation you should use.

The next stage is to build the calculation more carefully: how much corpus is required, how contributions may grow before retirement, and how withdrawals can be structured after retirement.
